## What keyword stuffing is

Keyword stuffing is repeating a word or phrase far beyond what the writing needs, in order to signal relevance to a search engine. Google's spam policies name it explicitly, and it has not worked for a long time.

Measuring it naively produces false positives. A product listing with fifty "Add to cart" buttons repeats those words fifty times without saying anything, and a page about socks will legitimately use the word "socks" a lot. So density alone is not the test.

## What squirrel checks

The rule runs on every crawled page over its rendered main copy. It uses the largest `<main>` or `role="main"` landmark with at least 100 words, or a sole `<article>` with that much copy. Otherwise it reads the body but leaves out site-wide header, navigation, footer and sidebar landmarks. An article or section keeps its own header, navigation, footer and aside. Scripts, styles, noscript, templates and code samples are excluded. Words contain three to 64 Unicode letters, so words such as `gemäß` and `Straße` stay whole. Han, Kana and Hangul runs are not scored because punctuation boundaries do not identify words in those scripts. It emits a single check named `keyword-stuffing`:

- Skipped when there is no `<body>`. Message: `No body content to analyze`.
- Skipped when most letters in the measured copy use scripts for which the rule has no function-word list, such as Cyrillic, Greek, Arabic, Hebrew, Devanagari or Thai. Message: `Most content uses a script without a function-word list`.
- Info below 100 words. Message: `Content too short for keyword density analysis`.
- Warn when any word crosses both thresholds. Message: `2 word(s) may be overused`, each item labelled `"widget" (4.7%)` with its count and density. Severity warning, weight 5.
- Pass otherwise. Message: `No keyword stuffing detected`.

A word is flagged when its density is above `density_threshold`, 3% by default, and its count is above `min_occurrences`, 5 by default. Two subtractions come first. English function words and generic verbs are excluded outright on every checked page, covering articles, pronouns, auxiliaries and words like `get` and `here` that read as high density on ordinary copy. The primary subtag of `<html lang>` selects an additional function-word list for German, Spanish, French, Portuguese, Italian or Dutch. English price-note terms (`incl`, `VAT`) are excluded only on an English page, and German price-note terms (`inkl`, `MwSt`, `zzgl`, `Versandkosten`) only on a German page. A page with no usable `lang` uses the English behavior. The `lang` attribute never disables the check by itself: a page that declares Russian but contains mostly Latin-script copy is still scored. After selecting the copy, the rule skips only when most measured letters use a script for which it has no function-word list. Then generic call-to-action labels on `<a>` and `<button>` elements are counted, and their words are subtracted from both the word's own count and the denominator, so a page of "Learn more" buttons is measured on its prose. Nested controls are counted once, from the outermost element.

Three more kinds of repeated text are not copy aimed at a term, and are subtracted too:

- **Function words in more languages.** The German list includes the formal and informal address and the common prepositions (`sie`, `für`, `dich`, `dir`, `deinen`, `ihn`, `ihm` and others; words under three characters are never counted at all), and `out` is treated as an English function word. A content word on the same page is still counted.
- **Link menus built without landmarks.** When a page has no main copy of its own to measure, blocks that are mostly link text (at least six links, at least four different labels, and at least 85% of the block's text inside links) are left out, as a mega menu made of plain `div` elements is, when they carry a real menu signal: `role="menu"`, `role="menubar"` or `role="navigation"`, or the `aria-haspopup` / `aria-expanded` attributes a dropdown sets, on the block or a wrapper up to three levels above it. Class and id names (`nav`, `menu`) and a plain `ul` of links are not signals, because a keyword spammer writes them too. A block where one word is in 30% or more of the labels ("emergency plumber london", "emergency plumber leeds", ...) is a keyword cloud and still counts, signal or not, as does a block repeating a single anchor. A real menu where most labels repeat a word ("Shoes sale", "Shoes new") therefore counts too, and a plain `div` menu with no signal does as well; both are accepted costs.
- **Rating and price text repeated once per card.** A short text that is the whole text of an element in at least three different parent wrappers, and whose rating- or price-shaped numbers differ from card to card (an amount with a currency symbol or code, a percentage, `4 of 5`, a count of stars or reviews), is a UI component and is not counted: "Average rating 4.5 of 5 stars", "from 12,90 € in instalments". A bare number is not such a number, a decimal included (`plumber 49.99`), because doorway spam numbers and prices its lines, and text with no such number, or with the same number every time, is never treated as a card, whatever wraps it. The deduction is bounded: at most 20% of the page's words in total (the most repeated texts first, clamped rather than skipped), never below half the density threshold for any one word (1.5% by default), and not at all for a word that is 10% or more of the page before it. A stuffed phrase dressed in a price can therefore still hide, down to that floor, below 10% of the page. At most 4,000 leaf elements (and 40,000 elements) are looked at per page: a card is about nine leaf elements, so a grid beyond roughly 400 cards is only deducted up to that point.

The last two are heuristics with a stated cost: a menu that uses none of those signals, or whose labels share a word, counts toward density, and card text with no number ("In stock"), a bare count ("12 items") or the same price on every card counts too. A hidden stuffed phrase is worse than a false positive, so the rule leans toward counting.

The site's own brand or product name is never flagged: a one-product landing page repeats its name because that is what the page is for. The name is read from the page's domain (`acme.com`, and launch-style variants such as `getacme.com` or `acmehq.io` resolve to `acme`), plus any word of `og:site_name` or `application-name` that the domain also carries. A site name the domain does not carry is not exempt, so a page cannot excuse a stuffed keyword by naming itself after it. Add other brand or product names to `whitelist`.

## How to fix it

Rewrite the passage. Use synonyms and related terms where the exact phrase is not needed, and write the sentence you would write if nobody were counting.

Add your brand name or a genuinely repeated product term to `whitelist` rather than editing around it. A page about one product will name that product often, and that is not stuffing.

## Options

| Option | Type | Default | Description |
|--------|------|---------|-------------|
| `density_threshold` | number | `3` | Keyword density percentage threshold |
| `min_occurrences` | number | `5` | Minimum word occurrences to flag |
| `whitelist` | array of strings | `[]` | Words to ignore (for example a brand name) |

### Configuration example

```toml squirrel.toml
[rule_options."content/keyword-stuffing"]
density_threshold = 4
whitelist = ["acme", "squirrelscan"]
```

## Enable / disable

### Disable this rule

```toml squirrel.toml
[rules]
disable = ["content/keyword-stuffing"]
```

### Disable all Content rules

```toml squirrel.toml
[rules]
disable = ["content/*"]
```

### Enable only this rule

```toml squirrel.toml
[rules]
enable = ["content/keyword-stuffing"]
```

`enable` replaces the default `["*"]`, so this is the only rule selected. Leave `disable` out: a rule that matches any `disable` pattern is skipped even when `enable` names it. Only an `enabled` setting under a rule's own `[rule_options]` table overrides both lists.
